FINANCING TERM SHEET — AI COMPUTE, SUMMER 2026

Cost of a gigawatt of compute, as the assembler of the racks estimates it: $47 billion.
Spot demand willing to fund it: none.
Structure borrowed from energy: take-or-pay offtake, bankruptcy-remote vehicles, contracted cash flow as collateral.
29 July, reported 10 August: data-centre bonds exempted from key securitisation rules.
10 August: a chipmaker signs memorandums of understanding with six asset managers "to mobilize over $500 billion of third-party capital." Its release describes its compute as having the "longest life."
Useful life of the collateral, on the displacement cycle: two to three years.
Tenor of the financing: no field.

Nobody funds a gigawatt on spot demand, so the contract structure has to precede the concrete. The energy industry's financing toolkit is being borrowed, piece by piece, by an industry whose assets last a tenth as long.

The structure is complete. The missing field is whether the asset can carry it.

## FINDING ONE: THE CONTRACT PRECEDES THE CONCRETE.

Utility project finance has one move. Pre-commit a buyer to fifteen or twenty years of output, and a capital-intensive plant becomes financeable. Multi-year demand commitments de-risk supply-side capex, turning a lumpy build into a bankable cash flow. The precondition is a rationed commodity with credit-worthy buyers. Compute had both before the financing platforms were announced.

The corporate version is a general primitive: a price-insensitive, high-volume buyer contracts firm supply directly from a producer, jumps the queue, and internalises a scarce input. Hyperscalers did it for electrons. The same move fits firm water, rare-earth offtake, and guaranteed memory allocation.

The failure mode sits in the same place. A purchase agreement works when supply is buildable on contract. For inputs with hard physical or permitting ceilings, a contract relabels the shortage instead of solving it.

## FINDING TWO: THE RECLASSIFICATION, NOT THE ASSET, UNLOCKS THE CAPITAL.

Isolate a long-lived asset with contracted cash flows in a separate legal entity, and its risk prices independently of the sponsor. A Chicago Booth analysis in August found that AI infrastructure "has mostly been financed off the consolidated balance sheets of the largest technology companies via project and construction financing, equipment-backed lending, leases, asset-backed securities, and, yes, private credit." It added: "There are even loans secured by GPUs, with the chips that run AI themselves pledged as collateral."

Separate the user of an asset from the owner of its financing, and the user grows faster than its credit rating permits while the owner earns a spread on contracted cash flow. The scale explains the pressure. The big hyperscalers "undertook approximately $380 billion of capital spending in 2025 and are expected to spend roughly double that this year, putting capital spending on course to overtake operating cash flow." Debt issuance, the same analysis noted, has risen in step.

Then the regulator moved. In a staff letter dated 29 July, answering a request from a law firm acting for issuers, the SEC put a major subset of data-centre securitisations outside the risk-retention and disclosure rules. It was reported on 10 August.

Since 1992 the SEC has treated "self-liquidating" to mean assets that "by their terms convert into cash within a finite time period." A data centre does not convert into cash that way, so the bonds tied to one fall outside the Exchange Act's definition of an asset-backed security, and the 5 percent risk-retention rule, the conflicts-of-interest rule and the periodic disclosure rules all fall away with the classification. The relief was granted because the collateral outlasts the paper. Finding Five is about what happens when it does not.

The reclassification, not the asset, unlocks the capital.

Risk retention existed because sponsors who keep none of the risk originate differently. Removing it does not remove the reason it existed.

A lease that has not commenced is a promise with a start date.

## FINDING THREE: THE VENDOR IS FINANCING THE PURCHASE OF ITS OWN PRODUCT.

The chipmaker's 10 August release is candid in its subtitle. The platforms "Turn NVIDIA Compute and Full-Stack AI Infrastructure Into an Investable Asset Class for Global Capital," with "Long-Duration Usage-Linked Revenue While Supporting NVIDIA's Ecosystem Growth Across Hardware Sales and Software Adoption." The partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR are memorandums of understanding.

That is the general shape of vendor financing: the vendor helps finance the customer's purchase of the vendor's product, which manufactures demand and books revenue.

It is a warning, not a template. The same loop that looks like ecosystem-building in the bull case looks like Enron in the bear case, and only hindsight separates them.

The practical diagnostic is dull and works: discount "committed" and "up to" figures against deployed cash, and read the gap as the size of the loop.

An MOU to mobilise over $500 billion is a sentence about intent.

The deployed figure is the sentence about money.

## FINDING FOUR: THE FORWARD CONTRACT DOES THE DUE DILIGENCE, IN PUBLIC.

A credible, capital-rich buyer signing a forward purchase converts technology risk into schedule risk, and schedule risk is financeable at far lower cost. In August, an initial agreement for Anthropic to buy roughly $250 million of chips that will not ship until 2027 put Fractile, a British startup, into advanced talks to raise at a $6.5 billion valuation, "more than six times its price in May."

The contract did the work of a late-stage diligence process, in public. This is standard in energy and aerospace, launch contracts and power purchase agreements, and was almost absent in deep-tech hardware until labs acquired both the balance sheets and a strategic motive to diversify their suppliers.

The failure mode is asymmetric. A commitment the buyer can walk away from cheaply costs the buyer little and costs the supplier's late-stage investors everything. A valuation built on one counterparty's option is fragile in exactly the way that option is valuable.

## FINDING FIVE: THE TENOR THAT WORKS FOR REACTORS IS POISON FOR GPUs.

The structure was designed for assets that last. The asset it now finances does not.

Compute depreciates on a cycle electricity does not have. The Chicago Booth analysis puts it plainly: GPUs "are being displaced every two or three years as faster ones are developed, and nobody yet knows what a used one is worth." A ten-year compute purchase agreement, were anyone to sign one, prices that displacement at zero, and the residual assumptions behind it are unvalidated across a full down-cycle. Toll roads do not get obsoleted by a firmware release.

You can be long the wrong megawatts.

Take-or-pay converts a demand shock into a solvency event for the buyer. If agent revenue disappoints, whoever signed multi-year compute commitments discovers they bought a liability, not a moat.

The vendor's release says its compute offers the "longest life." The lender should ask: longest compared with what, and measured how.

## FINDING SIX: THE MEMORY MAKERS LEARNED FROM THE LAST BUST.

Take-or-pay migrated from energy to memory for a reason the memory makers can recite. Suppliers burned by earlier boom-bust cycles will not add capacity on spot-price optimism. The three big memory makers are now locking customers into supply agreements running as long as five years, some with take-or-pay provisions "requiring customers to pay for committed volumes even if they ultimately take fewer chips." Long supply agreements shift volume risk to the buyer in exchange for price certainty, which is what makes the capacity financeable at all.

There is no forward market a mid-size buyer can use for either memory or firm power. Power has forward curves at the liquid hubs and no instrument at all for located, firm, long-duration supply, which is the only kind a data centre can take delivery of. Memory has none to begin with. A forward market would let a mid-size AI company hedge a quarter at a time instead of committing five years of volume, the way commodity buyers do. Until it exists, the contract is the hedge, and the contract is too long.

The supplier with the scars wrote the terms.

The buyer with the growth plan signed them.

Every structure in this essay moves risk to someone who can hold it longer: a vehicle, a lease, an insurer, an infrastructure fund.

None of them makes the asset last longer. It lasts about three years, whoever holds it.

## THE EXPLANATIONS AFTERWARD

The findings have their exits. Each gets its due.

The first explanation was that this is how every capital-intensive industry got built, and the alarm is naive.

Correct on the history. LNG terminals, pipelines and power plants were all financed this way, and Finding Six says the memory line will not get built any other way. The objection is right about the structure and silent about the tenor, and the tenor is the whole finding.

The second explanation was that the contracts are doing a job nothing else can, because there is no forward market.

Also correct, and it explains why the tenor is long. A supplier financing new capacity against one buyer's promise has no way to lay the risk off, so the promise has to cover the payback period of the fab or the plant. The tenor is set by the absence of a market, which makes it a fact about plumbing and not a view anybody holds about the asset. Build the market and the tenor falls on its own.

**Objection:** So sign the contract or do not.

**Reply:** Sign it for the tenor of the asset, not the tenor of the ambition. A reactor's is thirty years. A GPU's is three.

**Objection:** Nobody finances a plant on three-year offtake.

**Reply:** Nobody finances a reactor on three-year offtake. A GPU is not a reactor, and the platforms mobilising $500 billion are betting that a residual-value curve can be drawn for it. Somebody independent should draw it first.

The third explanation was that the leases and the offtake are contracted and therefore safe.

Contracted by whom, against what revenue. The dark-fibre collapse was contracted too, and it is the precedent the critics of circular financing invoke. Offtake assumes durable demand. Pre-committed contracts against demand that does not arrive are stranded liabilities, and they are stranded in a vehicle whose design lets the sponsor walk.

The fourth explanation was the inversion, and the vendor would make it: the chips last longer than the skeptics say. Older accelerators keep earning on inference long after they stop being frontier for training, so the useful life is longer than the capability curve implies, and long tenors are prudent, not reckless.

It is a coherent argument, and the demand for inference makes part of it plausible.

**Objection:** The chips last longer than the curve.

**Reply:** Show the curve.

The evidence that settles it is observable and mostly unpublished: the rental rate and resale price of accelerators three and four years after delivery, through at least one period when new supply is abundant. If a three-year-old accelerator still earns most of its original rate, long tenors are defensible and the inversion wins. The failure mode belongs in the same sentence: no residual curve for this asset class has been tested through a down-cycle, and a curve drawn in a shortage describes the shortage.

## THE CONTRACT AND THE 429

[power-ppa-forge](https://github.com/AthenaTheOwl/power-ppa-forge) is the offtake problem before the contract exists. Three hyperscalers want power off the same 1,000-megawatt reactor, and none will tell the others how much it needs or what it would pay, which are the two facts a lender wants. The repo is a mechanism for allocating the plant without forcing disclosure, which is the thing a compute purchase agreement needs and a spreadsheet cannot do.

[capital-build-reconciler](https://github.com/AthenaTheOwl/capital-build-reconciler) is the contract after the fact. You ask for tokens in one region and get a 429; the same region eats the only rate-limit hit of the month, half its calls bounced. The reconciler ranks regions by that refusal rate and points the worst one at the foundry, packaging and memory pillars it implies, which is the series a residual-value modeller would want and nobody currently reports.

Sign for the life of the asset.

Count deployed cash, not committed.
