EXIT RECORD — VOLUNTARY SEPARATION

Employee: the author. Tenure: four years, six weeks. Start: 25 July 2022. End: 2 September 2026.
Reason for leaving, as recorded: personal opportunity. Reason for leaving, as understood: below.
Rehire eligibility: yes, which is the funniest field on the form.
Exit interview: conducted. Questions asked about what went wrong: several. Questions asked about what would have kept me: none.

Destination: Chief of Technology and Science, carGO Health.

Decision date: March 2026, which makes the last six months a story about vesting schedules and not about deliberation.

This is the note the form has no field for. It is written in the register the rest of this site uses, which means it is mostly about incentives and loop times, and it is the only piece here where the explanations afterward are mine instead of someone else's.

·   ·

## FINDING ONE: A COMPENSATION NUMBER IS A FORECAST.

Every year for three years, my total compensation rose 15 to 20 percent. Generosity had nothing to do with it; that is the design: equity granted at hire vests on a back-loaded schedule, refreshers land on top of it, and the increases are what keeps a person in the seat until the four-year cliff clears.

In March I learned two things in the same week. There would be no promotion that year. And the next grant was nearly 250 units smaller, which, priced at the stock, projected to a pay cut of just under 15 percent for 2027.

A compensation number is a forecast of where the organisation expects you to be. Mine had been forecasting upward for three years and then forecast down.

I did not resign in March. I waited for the four-year vest, took a holiday in southern Africa, and handed in notice afterward, which is the least principled paragraph in this essay and the most honest one. The handcuffs are called golden because they work.

I would have left with or without a role to go to. The role came later.

## FINDING TWO: THE PEER BAR IS A SYSTEM PROPERTY, NOT A CULTURAL ONE.

There is a piece of folk advice that you become the average of the five people you spend the most time with. It is imprecise as psychology and accurate as an organisational model, because the five people are not chosen: they are assigned by the org chart, and the org chart is downstream of who stayed.

By early 2026, most of the people whose judgment had taught me something were gone or going, for reasons that were entirely rational for them. Post-layoff organisations have a selection problem that nobody manages explicitly: the people with the most options exercise them first, and the average of the remaining five drifts in a direction no one voted for.

That drift is measurable if anyone chooses to measure it, and nobody does, because the metric would be embarrassing and the fix is expensive.

I stopped learning from the room. That is a more disqualifying sentence than any complaint about process.

·   ·

The best hour of my four years happened at a whiteboard after nine at night, with two people, one of whom no longer works there, arguing about whether a constraint was physical or administrative.

It was administrative.

Nothing in the record contains that hour. There is no field for it, and if there were, the field would be scored.

## FINDING THREE: THE RANKINGS WERE NEVER RANDOM, ONLY INCOMPLETE.

A meritocracy is a claim about a measurement. It says that promotions, scope and money track delivered outcomes, and like any claim about a measurement it is either instrumented or it is decoration.

The ranking systems I worked inside were not arbitrary, and pretending otherwise would be the lazy version of this essay. They predicted individual performance under familiar evaluation with reasonable accuracy. People rated highly were usually faster, more polished and more reliable at the things the rubric had been built to see.

What they predicted poorly was team value. What they predicted worse was performance under novel conditions. What they barely predicted at all was who made the people around them more capable.

And they failed, repeatedly and in the same directions, to tell certain pairs apart.

Low capability from capability that needed a different condition to activate.

Weakness from asymmetry.

Caution from an accurate read of which decisions were irreversible.

Insubordination from asking whether an instruction's purpose had expired.

Dependence from distributed cognition.

Absent executive presence from leadership that did not require a permanent body in the room.

Laziness from the removal of unnecessary work.

Slowness from operating on a longer clock than the review cycle.

Failure to generalise from possession of a narrow talent worth building around.

The institution usually did observe the thing. That is the part people get wrong when they imagine this as blindness. The observation gets recorded, accurately, and then filed under a word from the deficiency vocabulary: inconsistent, too deep, not strategic, diffuse, low scope, needs to build relationships, difficult to place. The record contains the evidence. The taxonomy makes it sound like a disease.

Here is the question that would settle whether any of it was merit, and it is the same question this site asks of every other scoring system.

Was the audit ever run?

Take a year of promotion decisions. Take what those people delivered over the following two. Compare, and publish the correlation, or at minimum look at it once in private and tell somebody what you found.

Nobody ran it, and the reason is structural before it is sinister. Most institutions already pay people to observe performance. They do not reward those people for discovering that the institution's earlier judgment was wrong. Often the incentive runs the other way, because a revised classification is an admission and a defended one is a decision.

The fixes are unmysterious, and cheap set against what they save. Assessments that expire. Measures tagged with the context they were taken in. Team-uplift as a graded dimension rather than a soft comment. Appeals tied to evidence instead of to advocacy. People owning their own raw performance data. And the one that changes behaviour: rewarding a manager for discovering a mistaken classification instead of for defending it.

None of that is what I was told. What I was told was to invest in stakeholder management and relationships, which was accurate reporting of the observed allocator by people who had read it correctly. I have no quarrel with the messengers. In a system where the binding constraint is agreement across many owners, relationship work genuinely is the highest-return activity. In a system where you can put a hypothesis in front of evidence the same week, the scarce input is the hypothesis. Those are two different jobs and I am better at the second one.

I had written this argument down before I needed it, which is either evidence of consistency or evidence that I had been thinking about the exit for longer than I admitted. A [chapter of the fiction I write](https://archiveofourown.org/works/762170/chapters/247338966) is an institutional inquiry into exactly this: a school that ranks its students accurately by one measure, mistakes that measure for the person, and has to be shown its own records to see it. The fictional version got the line I could not improve on afterward.

The institution's failure was not usually cruelty. Sometimes it was certainty.

## FINDING FOUR: WHEN CONTRIBUTION IS UNMEASURED, PRESENCE BECOMES THE PRODUCT.

A leader told a room I was in to speak up in meetings, no matter what we said, so that people would know we were present.

I want to be fair to that advice. It was sincere, it was offered as help, and within the system it described it was correct. If the allocator cannot see contribution, it will fall back on the signals it can see, and the cheapest of those is evidence that you were in the room.

Follow that mechanism for a few years and it produces exactly what you would predict.

Process appears, and its function is to show that someone is managing something.

Escalation stops being a tool for unblocking work and becomes a way to register activity, because an escalation is legible in a way that quiet competence is not.

Meetings acquire attendees whose role in them is attendance.

And the arithmetic is worse than zero. A recurring meeting that occupies eight capable people for an hour costs eight hours whether or not anything is decided, and the person who called it is rewarded for the calling. An escalation that produces no decision consumes the attention of everyone named in it. Activity that generates no output is a withdrawal from other people's accounts, and the withdrawal never appears on the statement of the person making it.

Over enough cycles this becomes a selection effect, not a character flaw, and the distinction matters. If the system rewards visible activity, then the population at the upper levels gradually fills with people who are excellent at visible activity. That is not the same population as people who are excellent at the work. Nobody has to be cynical for this to happen. Each promotion is individually defensible.

The layoffs were supposed to correct it and did not, for the same reason. A reduction run against legible criteria removes the people whose contribution was hardest to see, which is disproportionately the ones doing unglamorous load-bearing work, and leaves intact the people whose principal skill is being visible. I watched a reduction pass through an organisation and make the ratio worse.

The bargain is the part that ended.

The trade I had signed up for was: tolerate the overhead, and in exchange get compensation that compounds, colleagues who raise your ceiling, and problems large enough to be worth the friction. In my last year the compensation curve inverted, the colleagues left, and the overhead grew. Three of three. At that point it is no longer a difficult decision, it is an arithmetic one, and I had been doing the arithmetic since March.

My honest view, offered as a view: the culture in the part of the business I worked in is past the point where an internal correction fixes it, because the people who would have to run the correction are selected by the thing that needs correcting. I might be wrong, and I would like to be. The falsification is straightforward, which is why I am willing to write it down.

If, within two years, the organisation publishes a promotion audit against delivered outcomes, or ties a level to measured uplift in the people around a person, or removes a layer whose output is coordination about coordination, then the correction is possible from inside and I called it too early.

It should come to a head eventually. It had not, by the time I left.

·   ·

Somewhere in year three I started building the evidence at night, because the loop I could control was the one that ran between my own hands and a test.

Ninety repositories, roughly. Nobody asked for any of them.

That is either the healthiest thing I did in four years or the clearest symptom, and I have stopped trying to decide which.

## FINDING FIVE: THE PRINCIPLES WERE RIGHT. THE PERFORMANCE OF THEM WAS THE ROT.

I should say plainly that I like the Leadership Principles. I liked them when I joined, and I like them now.

Read as written, they are a systems thinker's notes. "Leaders start with the customer and work backwards." Frugality: "Accomplish more with less. Constraints breed resourcefulness, self-sufficiency, and invention." Think Big: "Thinking small is a self-fulfilling prophecy." Insist on the Highest Standards: "Leaders are continually raising the bar." Deliver Results: "Leaders focus on the key inputs for their business." Dive Deep asks leaders to be "skeptical when metrics and anecdote differ."

Every one of those is about the structure behind an outcome. Work backwards from the person the system serves. Treat the constraint as a source of design. Watch the inputs, because the outputs are late. Distrust the metric when it disagrees with what you can see. I spent a thesis building a [system-dynamics model of Bitcoin](https://github.com/AthenaTheOwl/MIT-SDM-Thesis-on-System-Dynamics-Modeling-of-Bitcoin), all stocks, flows, delays and the feedback between adoption, supply and price expectation, and I recognised that way of thinking the first time I read the list.

What I watched happen to the principles was something else.

A principle that is applied is hard to see. It shows up months later as a decision that turned out well, with no author attached. A principle that is performed is visible at once: in the first paragraph of a document, in a meeting, in a promotion packet that names the principle beside the anecdote. The review cycle rewards what it can see, and so it rewarded the performance.

Customer obsession became a sentence at the top of documents that never met a customer.

Frugality became a story told in a meeting that cost eight people an hour.

Insist on the Highest Standards became a way to stop a proposal without engaging with it.

Deliver Results became a slide with the result on it.

The principles also became weapons. A principle cited in a review is hard to argue with in the room, which makes it useful for ending an argument its speaker had not won.

That is a loop, and it turns one way. Performing the principles is rewarded. The rewarded are promoted. The promoted set the example and run the next reviews, and the next cohort learns that the performance is the job. Each turn leaves fewer people applying the principles and more people displaying them, and from the inside it looks like a culture that talks about its principles constantly, which is how it looked.

The fault was never in the text. The principles assume a reader who will internalise them, carry them into decisions nobody watches, and be judged on those decisions. A system that grades what it can see will select for people who are good at being seen holding them. Finding Four describes the mechanism. This is what the mechanism did to the best thing the company ever wrote down.

## FINDING SIX: THE INSTRUMENT MEASURED TIMING, AND EVERYONE KNEW THE SCHEDULE.

The advice circulates openly, in the tone of someone passing on a tip about parking. Make sure the senior people see you shortly before calibration. Land something visible in the weeks beforehand. Do not do your best work in January.

Take that seriously for a second, because it is the most damaging sentence available about a review system, and it is said without embarrassment by people who are trying to help you.

If moving identical work from March to September changes the rating it receives, the instrument has stopped measuring the work and started measuring recall, and recall decays over months while the review cycle runs over a year.

That is a known exploit against a published schedule. In any other system on this site I would call it benchmark gaming and treat it as a hard defect: an evaluation with a documented exploit that nobody patches has stopped being an evaluation. Here it is simply folklore, traded between colleagues, stable for years, and mentioned in enough one-to-ones that pretending it is a rumour would be dishonest.

Two consequences follow, and the second is the one that actually corrodes things.

The first is allocative. Effort moves toward the pre-calibration window, so a fraction of the year's ingenuity goes into arrival time rather than into the work.

The second is cultural. Once the exploit is common knowledge, everybody has to decide whether to use it, and the people who refuse pay for the refusal out of their own rating. Cynicism in an organisation like that is the correct reading of the instrument, arrived at by observant people, and once enough of them arrive at it the performance becomes the job. Meetings acquire a visible-effort register. Updates acquire an audience. Work acquires a launch date chosen for the calendar rather than for readiness.

None of the individuals are behaving badly. Every one of them is optimising against the measurement they were handed, which is exactly what capable people do, and it is the oldest result in this field: you get what you measure, including when what you are measuring is who was standing nearby in August.

The patch is not mysterious. Write contributions down when they happen, timestamped, and calibrate from the record instead of from the room's memory. Every engineering organisation already knows how to do this, because it is what a changelog is.

Nobody wanted the changelog.

## FINDING SEVEN: THE BUSINESS I WORKED IN IS NOT THE BUSINESS THAT IS WINNING.

This part is public and checkable, which is why it is the only part with numbers that are not mine.

AMAZON, Q2 2026

Revenue: $200.6 billion, up 20 percent year over year.

Operating income: $27.5 billion, up 43 percent.

AWS: $42.2 billion, up 37 percent, with a reported backlog of $496 billion.

Full-year capex raised from roughly $200 billion to about $220 billion, attributed to higher memory costs.

AWS is about a fifth of the revenue and the reason the operating income line looks like that. The infrastructure position is real, the backlog is real, and I think the stock has runway on it.

Retail is the other four fifths, and it is where I worked.

Three things about it, in descending order of how checkable they are by someone who has never worked there.

The storefront. Open the site and count how many of the first twenty results are sponsored, how many are the same product under four names, and how many carry a brand you have heard of. It reads like a garage sale with a recommendation engine attached. Then price a basket of staples against Costco and Walmart, both of which now win often enough that price leadership is no longer the reason to start there. The remaining reasons are delivery speed and the catalogue, and the catalogue is mostly third-party sellers, which means the durable business is increasingly fulfilment and advertising, not retail judgment about what to stock.

The decision systems. This is the part I can speak to from inside and the part a reader has to take on trust: the forecasting, buying and allocation systems were once genuinely ahead of the field, and by 2026 the frontier had moved and they had not moved with it. What innovation there is happens in logistics, in the physical network, where it is real and impressive. The layer that decides what to buy, at what price, to put where, is running on ideas that were excellent when they were built.

The vocabulary. The gap between "we are a Day 1 company" and the observable cadence of decisions had grown wide enough to be a running joke among people who liked the place. That is the cheapest possible tell. When the language a company uses about itself has to be defended instead of demonstrated, the language has become the product.

A company can be enormously successful and still have one division coasting on a moat built a decade ago. Those two facts sit together comfortably. They sat badly with my next four years.

## FINDING EIGHT: THE MOST EXPENSIVE WORK WAS THE WORK NOBODY PROPOSED.

Every audit of a slow organisation counts the things that went wrong. The larger number is the things that were never attempted, and it is invisible by construction, because an idea that dies before it is written leaves no artifact to count.

The arithmetic each person runs is simple and rational. Expected value of the idea, multiplied by the odds it survives the process, minus the cost of getting it through. When the third term grows, ideas below a certain size stop being worth proposing, and the threshold is not published anywhere. Each individual is making a correct local decision.

I know roughly what my own threshold became, because I can see it from outside now. By year four I was not proposing anything that would take less than a quarter to matter, because a quarter was the minimum unit the process could digest. Every idea smaller and faster than that went into a repository instead, at night, where the loop was mine.

That is the silent failure, and it has a property that should frighten anyone running a large organisation: it accelerates. The higher the proposal cost, the larger the minimum viable idea, and the larger the minimum viable idea, the fewer people can originate one, and the fewer people originate, the more the system relies on the few who can, which raises everyone else's proposal cost again.

Nobody files a report about the thing they did not say. There is no incident review for an unwritten document.

## FINDING NINE: THE CALENDAR WAS THE REAL PERFORMANCE REVIEW.

Makers need contiguous hours. Managers need slots. A calendar that serves the second cannot serve the first, and the default settings of a large organisation serve the second, because slots are what coordination consumes.

The measurement I wish I had run, and did not, is embarrassingly easy. Take a week. Count the contiguous blocks of ninety minutes or more with no meeting in them. Track the number across a year and across levels.

I would predict two things from that chart. That the number falls as seniority rises, in a job family where the work is supposed to get more technical. And that it falls fastest for the people whose calendars are open to other people's booking, which is a proxy for agreeableness rather than for the value of the interruption.

A calendar is a budget that spends itself. Nobody has to approve the expenditure, the person being spent is not consulted, and the spending is invisible in the review, which measures output while the input was being taken.

By the end I was doing the actual work in three places: the two hours before anyone else was awake, the flight time, and the evenings that produced the repositories. That is a system telling you where it thinks your work belongs.

## FINDING TEN: THE COMPANY HAD EVERYTHING THE FRONTIER NEEDED EXCEPT THE LOOP.

This is the finding that made the decision personal rather than financial, and it is the one this whole site is about.

Consider what the place actually had. Demand signal at a scale almost nobody else can buy. Decades of transaction and logistics history. Physical infrastructure to test against. Capital that does not blink at a research bet. And people, still, who were genuinely excellent at applied science.

Every input a serious applied-AI operation needs, sitting in the same building.

What it did not have was a loop time that matched the technology. Model capability moved quarterly. Inference cost fell about ten times a year at constant capability. The cost of trying an idea fell to an afternoon. The apparatus for deciding whether an idea was worth trying still ran on a planning cadence designed when experiments cost a quarter of engineering time, and the apparatus did not notice that its own unit economics had changed.

The result is the control-theory signature the essay beside this one describes in power grids: the forcing loop sped up, the corrective loop did not, and the gap is where the opportunity goes.

I watched proposals that could have been answered by a two-day experiment go into a document cycle instead, and come out the other side four months later as a smaller proposal, correctly reasoned, about a world that had moved.

Nobody was stupid. Everybody was on the clock they were given.

·   ·

I spent the last of the handcuffs on three weeks in southern Africa, where a great deal of very old machinery runs correctly without a review cycle and nothing there was waiting for my document.

I thought about work exactly twice.

Both times it was about the feedback loop.

## FINDING ELEVEN: NOBODY COMPUTED THE RETENTION PRICE.

Here is the part that still puzzles me as a mechanism-design problem.

Replacing a senior person costs recruiting, a hiring loop, ramp time, the projects that stall while the seat is empty, and the institutional memory that leaves with them. Estimates vary; the number is not small, and every large employer has it somewhere in a deck.

The price of keeping one is usually a fraction of that. In my case it was a promotion decision, or a grant that did not invert, or a problem large enough to be worth the friction. Any one of the three would probably have bought another two years.

Nobody asked which one it was. Not in a retention conversation, not in the exit interview, which asked what had gone wrong and never asked what the price would have been.

An organisation that can forecast demand for ten million items to the week does not have a technology problem here. It has an incentive problem: the cost of replacing me lands on a budget line nobody defends, months later, and the cost of keeping me would have landed on a compensation pool someone defends today.

That is a mechanism, not a mistake, and it is why the exit interview is always the wrong instrument. It is run after the decision, by the party that lost, about the decision they already lost.

## FINDING TWELVE: THE STRONGEST OBJECTION TO THIS ESSAY IS MINE.

Every argument here rests on one assumption: that my contribution was real and the system failed to see it.

If that assumption is wrong, this is an ordinary story about someone who was rated accurately and did not enjoy the rating.

So the honest accounting.

I was genuinely mediocre at making my work legible to people who were not in it. I optimised for being right over being read, and I knew I was doing it, and I did it anyway, partly out of impatience and partly because the alternative felt like theatre. That is a real skill I chose not to build, and choosing not to build a skill has a price. I paid it. Nobody cheated me.

What I will not grant is that legibility and contribution are the same measurement. A person who does both is more valuable than a person who does one. A system that cannot distinguish them will price them identically, and that is the finding, not a grievance.

And because this site's entire argument is that a claim needs a checkable artifact: the evidence is published. Roughly ninety repositories. A weekly brief with a citation gate that fails the build when a number cannot be traced to a source, which has caught my own fabrications more than once. Typed gates, eval harnesses, replay tooling, a scorecard that forces a retire bucket so a quiet week cannot hide a dead project.

Read it and decide. That is the only version of this claim I am entitled to make, and it is deliberately the one that can go against me.

·   ·

The mechanism that finally did it was not the compensation, the peer bar, the rankings, the meetings, the business, the loop, or the retention price.

It was that I had stopped being surprised.

Four years is long enough to learn a system's grammar. After that, every outcome is a sentence you could have written yourself, and the only question left is whether you want to keep reading it.

I did not.

## THE EXPLANATIONS AFTERWARD

Eleven theories were offered to me, or offered to myself. Each gets its due, and several are right.

The first explanation was that this is about money.

Partly, and saying so beats pretending otherwise. More precisely, it is about what the money said. A 15 percent projected cut alongside no promotion is not a compensation event, it is a forecast, and the forecast was legible.

The second explanation was that I should have changed the system from inside.

People do, it is real work, and it takes years. That is a coherent career and I respect the people doing it. It is not the one where you find out whether the thing you keep building works.

**Objection:** So you left to avoid the slow work.

**Reply:** I left to take on the other slow work, which is a company. Both are slow. Only one of them lets me set the clock.

The third explanation was that every large company works this way, so none of this is remarkable.

Most large companies do allocate partly on visibility, and if that were the whole claim it would be a shrug. The difference is that this one writes the other version down, teaches it, and recites it, and people believe it because it is stated with conviction by people who also believe it.

That belief is the expensive part. It is what keeps a capable person waiting a year for a correction the system has no mechanism to produce, and then another year, because surely the measurement is real and this cycle was the anomaly.

A company that said "we promote the people who are visible to the people who decide" would lose nothing in performance and would save its best people a great deal of time.

The fourth explanation was that everyone is starting companies, so this is market timing.

Possibly. Two central banks hiked into an oil shock the month I left, the AI trade split into legs that move against each other, and the queue for anything physical runs five years. That is a strange moment to leave a balance sheet. It is also the moment when the constraints are legible enough to build against.

The fifth explanation was that I was passed over and everything above is the rationalisation.

It is the right question to ask and I cannot fully clear myself of it, because nobody can. What I can do is note the order of events. The apparatus on this site, the briefs, the gates, the eval harnesses, predates the March review by more than a year. The argument about rankings measuring what they were built to measure was written into a piece of fiction before it was written into a resignation. Whether that is consistency or a long-running rationalisation is genuinely for a reader to judge, which is why I have dated everything.

The sixth explanation was that I benefited from the same visibility system I am criticising.

Yes. Three years of 15 to 20 percent increases did not arrive because a mechanism measured my uplift. They arrived because the system worked for me, and I did not write an essay about its measurement properties while it was working. That defends neither the system nor me. It is the reason I have tried to write this as a description of a mechanism instead of a complaint about a verdict.

The seventh explanation was that the coordination work I am dismissing is real work, and I could not see it because it was never aimed at me.

This is the strongest objection in the list and I want to give it the room it deserves. Large organisations genuinely do need people who hold context across teams, absorb conflict, translate between functions, and keep a decision alive across quarters. Much of that work is invisible by construction: when it goes well, nothing happens, which is the same signature as doing nothing at all.

I am not claiming every coordinator was surplus. I am claiming the system could not tell the difference between the ones who were and the ones who were not, and that when a category cannot be measured, it expands. The test I would apply is the same one I would apply to an agent: remove the role for a quarter and see what breaks. Nobody runs that test either.

The eighth explanation was that a smaller company will have worse versions of all of this.

Many will, and some of the failure modes get sharper rather than softer: less process means less recourse, a founder's judgment substitutes for a rubric, and a bad peer bar in a room of twelve has nowhere to hide. I am not claiming to have escaped organisational failure. I am claiming to have exchanged a slow, well-documented, self-defending version of it for a fast, undocumented, correctable one, and that I would rather debug the second.

The ninth explanation was the inversion, which I owe the essay: what would have to be true for the organisation to have been right about me?

It is not a hard case to make. That my contribution was real but narrower than I think. That the work I dismissed as coordination theatre was holding together a machine whose complexity I never had to carry. That my unwillingness to invest in being read was not a principled stand but an expensive preference I dressed as one, and that a system correctly discounted a person who would not do a core part of the job.

I can construct that case without strain, which is why the audit question in Finding Three matters so much to me. If the data existed, it would settle which of us was right. It does not exist. In its absence I have my evidence, published, and they have a rating, unpublished, and a reader can weigh those however they like.

The tenth explanation was that this is a downturn, not a culture.

Genuinely possible, and it is the confound I cannot separate. The layoffs, the flat compensation pool, the risk aversion and the shortened horizon all arrive together in a bad macro year, and every one of my findings has an innocent version in which the organisation was simply doing what organisations do when money tightens. The test is what happens on the other side. If the peer bar recovers, the promotion audit appears, and the proposal cost falls when the cycle turns, then I mistook weather for climate. I do not expect that, but I have written down what would change my mind, which is more than the counter-argument has offered me.

The eleventh explanation was that I am being ungrateful.

I was paid well to learn from an unusually good machine for four years, and some of the colleagues were the best I have worked with. Both of those are true, and neither obliges me to describe a declining peer bar as a growth opportunity. Gratitude and an accurate exit interview are compatible. This is the accurate version, minus the names.

## WHAT CARRIES OVER

Three things, in order of how often I use them.

Write it down before you argue about it. Whatever else a six-page document does, it converts a meeting into a diffable artifact, and I have not found a better technology for that at any company size.

The bar exists so the answer does not depend on who is in the room. Every gate in every repository on this site is that idea, ported. A linter that fails a banned phrase is a bar raiser that never gets tired and never gets promoted.

And the failure modes are worth more than the successes. The pre-mortem ledger here, which asks the largest position a rude question once a month and keeps score, is a mechanism I learned from an organisation that made me name what would go wrong before it went wrong.

Four years, six weeks, and a great many documents.

The mechanisms were right about most of it.

The forecast was the part I believed.
