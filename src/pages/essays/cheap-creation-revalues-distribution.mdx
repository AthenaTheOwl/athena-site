UPLOAD LOG — ONE STREAMING SERVICE, PEAK DAYS, JUNE 2026

Fully AI-generated tracks delivered per day: nearly 90,000.
Share of all new uploads: more than half.
Share of listening those tracks received, as of April: between 1 and 3 percent of streams.
Share of streams on such tracks found fraudulent in 2025: up to 85 percent.
Recommendations those tracks receive: none; excluded by policy.
Who audits the filter that decided: no field.

That is Deezer's own account of its catalogue this summer. On its busiest days, half the new supply was made by machines, and the platform decided that almost none of it would be put in front of a listener.

The last line is where the value moved.

## FINDING ONE: SUPPLY WENT VERTICAL.

The numbers change so fast that each announcement is out of date by the next. In April Deezer reported that fully AI-generated uploads had "increased from 10,000 to 75,000 in little over a year," or 44 percent of daily deliveries. In July it reported "90,000 AI-generated tracks per day," more than half of all new uploads at peak.

Games show the same curve. One industry report counted 225,000 new games released worldwide in 2025, "an average of 617 new games going online per day."

When the cost of making one more unit falls toward zero, the number of units stops being limited by the makers. It is limited only by how many can be uploaded.

## FINDING TWO: ATTENTION DID NOT.

A listener's day did not get longer. Deezer reports that fully AI-generated music accounts for "between 1-3%" of streams, and that most of even that listening was not listening: "up to 85% of the streams generated by fully AI-generated tracks were in fact fraudulent in 2025."

So the ratio that matters is uploads per hour of human attention, more than uploads per day, and it is rising as fast as the upload count.

When supply grows and attention stays fixed, the scarce good is no longer the thing being made. It is the slot in front of the person: the recommendation, the playlist, the front page, the shelf.

A bookshop with a million titles has the same window it had with a thousand.

The window was always the business.

Now everyone can see it.

## FINDING THREE: THE DISTRIBUTOR BECAME THE FILTER.

Deezer did not respond by improving AI music or banning it. It took control of the shelf. It says it became "the first streaming platform to detect, tag and exclude AI-generated music from algorithmic recommendations," and that its detection tool lets it exclude such tracks "from algorithmic and editorial recommendations." It will now "take down generative-AI tracks used to generate fraudulent streams, as well those that haven't been streamed for 6 months or more."

Each of those is a distribution decision. The upload is free. Being recommended is not, and the platform decides who gets it.

Deezer is also "licensing its AI-detection tech" to the rest of the industry. The filter has become a product in its own right.

## FINDING FOUR: LISTENERS CANNOT TELL, AND WANT TO BE TOLD.

Deezer's own survey found that "97% of people couldn't hear the differences between AI and human made music," and that 80 percent want fully AI-generated music labelled for listeners.

That combination moves another job to the distributor. If listeners cannot detect the difference themselves and want to know, someone between the maker and the listener has to detect it and say so. Provenance, which an earlier essay here argued is becoming a market, is being provided at the shelf.

## FINDING FIVE: CHEAP CREATION DID NOT SAVE THE CREATION TOOL.

The most famous tool for cheap video did not survive the year. In March OpenAI announced it would shut down Sora, and Disney ended the partnership that had included plans for a $1 billion stake. Disney's statement said it respected "OpenAI's decision to exit the video generation business."

The creation was cheap for the user and not for the host. NBC News recalled that Sora's head had earlier limited how many videos users could generate "due to the limited supply of computer chips available to power the video-generation model."

A creation tool is a producer. When production is the abundant part of a market, being the best producer is a weaker position than it looks, and a producer with a large compute bill is weaker still.

## FINDING SIX: THE MAKERS ARE PAYING TOO.

The people whose work the tools copy are counting a different cost. In GDC's 2026 survey of more than 2,300 game-industry professionals, "over half (52%)" think generative AI is having a negative effect on the industry, "up from 30% last year and 18% the year prior." About 7 percent think its effect is positive. And "over one-third (36%)" use generative AI tools in their work anyway.

Use and approval are moving in opposite directions. One reading of the split: makers use the tools because production costs are falling for everyone, and they dislike them because falling production costs are exactly what makes their own work less scarce.

## FINDING SEVEN: DISTRIBUTION MOVED INTO THE APPS PEOPLE ALREADY OPEN.

Software is repeating the pattern. Poke, an AI assistant launched in March, lets people use it "through their messaging platform of choice, whether that's iMessage, SMS, Telegram, or, in some markets, WhatsApp." The coding company Cognition bought it in a deal valuing it in the "low nine figures", for its interaction model and personality.

TechCrunch read the deal as evidence that "how AI assistants interact with users will become just as valuable as the underlying models powering them." The same logic holds for placement. An assistant inside the thread a person already has open gets asked more often than a better one behind a new install.

When capability is abundant, how often something is asked is the constraint, and that is decided by where it sits.

## THE EXPLANATIONS AFTERWARD

Each finding has a simpler reading. Each one gets heard.

The first explanation was that quality will sort this out, and good work will find its audience.

It will, through a distributor. Sorting a hundred thousand daily uploads is the distributor's work, and whoever does the sorting holds the value this essay is about.

The second explanation was that this is a fraud problem, not a distribution problem.

Much of it is fraud, by Deezer's count. But fraud is what cheap creation looks like when distribution pays per stream: if uploading is free and a stream pays, someone will upload to collect streams. The fraud is a symptom of the new cost structure.

The third explanation was that makers will go direct and own their audiences.

Some will. The direct channels are mostly rented: messaging apps, social feeds and app stores whose owners can change the rules.

**Objection:** Then own the audience.

**Reply:** On whose platform?

The fourth explanation was the inversion, and an independent curator would make it. "Distribution is revalued" means the incumbents win. The platforms that already own the feed absorb the curation layer themselves, and every third-party curation or provenance tool is squeezed out. On that reading, cheap creation does not create a new market in distribution. It hands the existing distributors more power over more supply.

The upload log supports it. Deezer detects, tags, excludes and removes on its own authority, and nobody outside Deezer audits the result.

**Objection:** Then the filter should be shared.

**Reply:** And inspectable.

The evidence that settles it is whether filtering becomes shared infrastructure or stays inside each platform. Deezer licensing its detector to others points one way. If detection, labelling and demotion become services that any distributor can buy and any maker can check, the value in distribution is spread out. If each platform's filter stays private, the value concentrates, and a maker's only appeal is to the company that decided.

## ONE STORY, THREE FORMATS

The author of this site has run the experiment on a small scale. Starforge Canticles is a serialised novel published chapter by chapter on Royal Road, and three repositories turn it into other things. [starforge-narrative-tools](https://github.com/AthenaTheOwl/starforge-narrative-tools) converts the prose into game script: 66 released Act 1 prose files, 39 mapped speakers, and validation gates on the output. [starforge-renpy-demo](https://github.com/AthenaTheOwl/starforge-renpy-demo) renders Act 1 as a Ren'Py narrative game with 2,414 labels, 264 menus and 14 fights. [starforge-rpg-prototype](https://github.com/AthenaTheOwl/starforge-rpg-prototype) builds the same act as a Godot RPG with seven playable crew, 13 quests and 23 dialogue trees.

Making the second and third formats got cheap enough for one person to do. None of them changed where readers come from. They still find the story through one platform's front page, which is this essay's argument at the scale of a single author.

No repository on this site measures distribution. The smallest useful one would track a single ratio for a category: new units supplied per unit of attention received, and what share of that attention the platform's own filter decided. For music, both numbers are already published. That ratio is the price of the shelf, and it is rising.

Make whatever you like.

Own the window it is seen through.

UPLOAD LOG — REMAINING FIELDS

Uploads per hour of listening: published, month by month.
Filter decisions: logged, with the reason for each exclusion.
Appeal: a maker can ask why a track was excluded, and get an answer.
Filter audit: by someone other than the platform that filters.

The upload costs nothing now. The window has a price, and the platform sets it alone.
