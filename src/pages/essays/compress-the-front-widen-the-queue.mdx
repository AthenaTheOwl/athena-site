PIPELINE LEDGER — AI-ENABLED DRUGS, 2026

Entered human trials: 117 assets, from 63 companies.
Completed Phase 1: 60, or 51.3 percent.
Completed Phase 2: 8, or 6.8 percent.
Median time from company founding to Phase 1: 6.5 years.
Time saved by AI in the stages after Phase 1: no field.

Those counts come from an analysis presented at the American Society of Clinical Oncology meeting this year, as tallied in IntuitionLabs' review of the AI-drug pipeline. Every line on the ledger is a measurement of the front of drug development.

The empty line is the back, where the money and the years are.

## FINDING ONE: THE FRONT GOT FAST.

The work AI does best in drug development sits at the start: nominating a target, generating candidate molecules, optimising a lead. Those steps were already the cheaper and quicker part of the pipeline, and they are the part that has compressed. Insilico reports a typical 13 months from programme start to development candidate, against a conventional figure it puts at 4.5 years, and rentosertib reached the start of Phase 1 in under 30 months. The figures are the vendor's.

The ledger shows what came out the other side. Of 117 AI-enabled assets that reached human trials, 60 had completed Phase 1. Only 8 had completed Phase 2.

Part of that gap is timing. Many of these assets entered trials recently, and Phase 2 takes longer than Phase 1. Timing explains the gap and is also the gap: the stage that takes longer is the stage where they are waiting.

## FINDING TWO: THE BACK IS SET BY BIOLOGY.

The first AI-designed drug with a real mid-stage readout shows what the back of the pipeline looks like. Insilico Medicine's rentosertib, a TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, was tested in a Phase 2a trial that randomised 71 patients across 22 sites in China for 12 weeks.

The primary endpoint was safety, and the trial met it: treatment-emergent adverse events ran at similar rates across all four arms. Lung function was secondary and favoured the high dose, a mean FVC gain of 98.4 mL (95 percent confidence interval, 10.9 to 185.9) against a mean decline of 20.3 mL on placebo. The safety data were less tidy. Seven patients stopped treatment because of liver toxicity, and 67 percent of the high-dose group completed the 12 weeks, against 88 percent on placebo.

The next step is the size the next step always is. Insilico registered a 320-patient Phase III study in July. It lists 47 hospitals and a primary completion date of October 2029. However the molecule was found, the trial that decides it needs patients, sites and time, and none of those came from a model.

## FINDING THREE: A FASTER FRONT WIDENS THE QUEUE.

Manufacturing learned this long ago. Eliyahu Goldratt's theory of constraints, introduced in his book The Goal, rests on one premise: a system's throughput "is limited by at least one constraint," and "Only by increasing flow through the constraint can overall throughput be increased." Its first step is to identify the constraint. Its third is to "Subordinate everything else" to it.

Drug development's constraint is not the front. Speeding up target nomination in a pipeline whose back is fixed does not shorten the pipeline. It sends more candidates, sooner, into the same slow stages, and the queue in front of those stages gets longer.

The ledger's two percentages are that queue drawn as numbers.

Widen the on-ramp and the bridge does not notice.

The traffic arrives sooner and waits longer.

## FINDING FOUR: EVEN THE ACCELERATOR POINTS AT THE FRONT.

The FDA is now taking applications for an Expedited IND Pilot, part of an initiative it calls Operation TrialBlazer. The program is "designed to evaluate new approaches for accelerating first-in-human (FIH) clinical trials." Qualified research institutions partner with sponsors to provide recommendations on "pharmacology and toxicology, clinical, and chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) components of first-in-human IND submissions," reviewed on a rolling basis.

It is a sensible programme, and it is aimed at the entrance. It shortens the path into Phase 1, which is the stage the ledger shows the AI companies already clearing. It does not add a patient or a site to Phase 2.

When the government builds an accelerator for the stage that is already fast, the constraint has not been identified yet.

## FINDING FIVE: THE THESIS QUIETLY CHANGED.

The early promise of AI drug discovery was better molecules: candidates that would succeed more often in the trials that matter. The claim now heard more often is different. AI-native companies can afford more shots on goal per dollar, because their early programmes cost less.

Those are two theses with different tests. The first needs a higher Phase 2 success rate than the industry's history. The second needs only a cheaper Phase 1. Rentosertib is evidence for the first. It is also one trial of 71 patients over 12 weeks, which makes it a hypothesis, not a result.

The second thesis is easier to prove and runs straight into Finding Three. More shots at a fixed bottleneck is more traffic at the bridge.

## FINDING SIX: REMOVING THE NEEDLE BEAT IMPROVING THE DRUG.

Two of the most notable recent approvals did not come from new mechanisms. They came from changing how an existing mechanism is taken.

In July the FDA approved the first oral PCSK9 inhibitor. Its announcement explains the whole story in one sentence: "PCSK9 inhibitors have previously been available only as injectable therapies." Merck's Lipfendra "is administered as a once-daily oral tablet."

The weight-loss market went the same way. Novo Nordisk's Wegovy pill, approved in December 2025 as "the first and only oral GLP-1 for weight loss in adults," produced an average weight loss of 16.6 percent against 2.7 percent on placebo if patients stayed on treatment. In June, Novo Nordisk reported that prescriptions for the pill had passed 3 million in just over five months.

Where adoption is limited by the inconvenience of taking something and not by how well it works, removing the inconvenience reaches far more people than improving the payload. That is a lesson about constraints too. The needle was the bottleneck, and nobody needed a better molecule to remove it.

## FINDING SEVEN: THE CONSTRAINT MOVES. IT DOES NOT DISAPPEAR.

A constraint that is removed in one place reappears in another. The Wegovy pill is a 25 mg daily tablet of the same molecule sold as a 2.4 mg injection, so each daily tablet carries more than ten times the drug of an injection dose. Removing the needle moves the bottleneck toward making enough of the active ingredient, and toward the payers who must fund many more patients.

The theory of constraints builds this into its method. Its fifth step is a warning: when a constraint has been broken, go back to the first step and find the next one.

Any plan that promises to remove friction should name where the friction goes next.

An airport that doubles its check-in desks and keeps one runway has built a larger departure lounge.

The passengers are grateful for the seats.

They still leave one plane at a time.

## THE EXPLANATIONS AFTERWARD

Each finding has a hopeful reading. Each gets its turn.

The first explanation was that AI will compress the back of the pipeline too, through better trial design and patient selection.

It may. The test for that claim is Phase 2 and Phase 3 success rates for AI-originated assets compared with the industry's history, and trial durations compared with trials for conventionally discovered drugs. A count of Phase 1 entries tests nothing here. The first such numbers put AI-originated assets at the industry's historical Phase 2 rate. Until that moves, the compression is a forecast.

The second explanation was that the low Phase 2 completion rate is only a matter of time.

Much of it is. The 6.8 percent will rise as the cohort ages, and the rate at which it rises is the measurement: climbing at the pace of ordinary Phase 2 durations means the queue was time, and stalling below the industry's history means the queue was holding candidates that should not have been sent. Either way the wait stays where it is.

The third explanation was that more shots on goal is a perfectly good strategy.

It is, while each shot is cheap and the goal is wide. But the expensive stages draw on trial sites and eligible patients, and those do not multiply because more candidates arrive. More shots from every company compete for the same trial capacity.

**Objection:** Then run more trials.

**Reply:** With whose patients?

The fourth explanation was the inversion, and a portfolio manager at a drug company would make it. The widened queue is the value. The theory of constraints says to exploit the constraint, which means never letting the scarce stage work on a weak input. A company with ten candidates at the door of Phase 2 can send the best one through. A company with one candidate must send whatever it has. On that reading, AI's contribution is not speed at the front but selection for the back: better shots, chosen from more.

The case is strong, and it turns the essay's own tool against it.

**Objection:** Then the queue is a feature.

**Reply:** If someone is choosing from it.

The evidence that settles it is the Phase 2 success rate of AI-originated assets against the historical rate for the same indications. First estimates exist. A 2024 BCG analysis, relayed in the same review, puts AI-discovered molecules at 80 to 90 percent success in Phase 1 and about 40 percent in Phase 2, which is roughly the industry's long-run rate; the rentosertib paper reports "similar levels of phase 2 trial failure as non-AI-discovered drugs." On those numbers the queue is a waiting room and the front end is filling it. If the rate climbs as the 117 mature, the selection reading earns its case.

## THE ASSIGNMENT AT THE BOTTLENECK

[dispatch-optimizer](https://github.com/AthenaTheOwl/dispatch-optimizer) is a logistics simulator, not a drug-development tool, but it studies the same problem at small scale. Orders arrive over time, drivers carry different capabilities, and a route evaluator decides whether an assignment can be served. Two policies run on the same world: a greedy one that takes the next feasible pair, and a batch assignment that solves a full cost matrix for each epoch.

Neither sees future orders. The repository asks where the simple dispatcher starts losing ground. Any constrained stage has to ask the same thing: when the scarce resource is fixed, the policy that decides what it works on next matters more than how fast new work arrives.

Find the stage everything waits for.

Spend the new speed there.

PIPELINE LEDGER — REMAINING FIELDS

Phase 2 success rate, AI-originated assets: first estimate about 40 percent, level with the historical rate. Pending for the 117.
Trial duration after Phase 1: measured, not assumed.
Selection at the bottleneck: which candidates went through, and why.
Where the constraint moved: named in advance.

The front of the ledger is full. The back is still being written, at the speed of patients.
