TERM SHEET — ONE PROPOSED STAKE

Holder: the United States government, through a sovereign wealth vehicle.
Stake: 5 percent of OpenAI, with the same offered for each leading US AI company.
Value at the March valuation: roughly $42.6 billion.
Model: the Alaska Permanent Fund.
Status: conceptual and early-stage; could require Congressional approval.
What the holder does when the company it owns needs regulating: no field.

In July, CNBC reported the Financial Times account: OpenAI "has proposed handing the U.S. government a 5% stake in the company" as it "seeks to defuse mounting political pressure in Washington." The stake would be held through a sovereign wealth vehicle, and Washington would hold the same share of the other leading labs.

Every line on the term sheet is about the money. The empty one is about the government's other job.

## FINDING ONE: A RENT THAT IS VISIBLE GETS CLAIMED.

The model named in the proposal was built for oil. Alaska's Permanent Fund, "Established in 1976," exists to preserve "Alaska's nonrenewable mineral and oil wealth as a renewable financial resource for current and future generations of Alaskans." Decrypt's account of the proposal describes it as a fund built to invest surplus oil revenue and "pay annual dividends to state residents."

The mechanism transfers easily. When a windfall accrues to a few visible owners, a public equity stake shares the upside without taking over the asset. Buying the public a claim is faster than fighting over the rules, and it is easier to explain than a new tax.

The AI labs have made their rent visible. Valuations measured in hundreds of billions are exactly the kind of number that invites a claim.

## FINDING TWO: THE OFFER CAME FROM THE COMPANY.

The unusual part of this proposal is who made it. CNBC reported that the pitch "came after more than a year of talks about a possible government stake in the company," with the chief executive first raising the idea with the administration in early 2025.

Not every lab was party to it. A source told CNBC that the administration and Anthropic had not discussed a government stake in Anthropic.

A company that offers the state a share of itself is sharing a windfall and also buying something: goodwill, a stake-holder's interest in its survival, and a seat for itself inside the government's calculation of what to regulate. The equity is a price, and the buyer is the company.

## FINDING THREE: THE PRECEDENT IS ALREADY ON THE BOOKS.

The United States already owns a large piece of a strategic technology company. Under the agreement Intel announced last year, the government agreed to buy "433.3 million primary shares of Intel common stock at a price of $20.47 per share, equivalent to a 9.9 percent stake in the company," funded by CHIPS Act grants that had been awarded but not yet paid.

The terms show how such a stake is meant to behave. The investment "will be a passive ownership, with no Board representation." The government "agrees to vote with the Company's Board of Directors on matters requiring shareholder approval, with limited exceptions." And it holds a warrant for another five percent, "exercisable only if Intel ceases to own at least 51% of the foundry business."

Passive, aligned with management, and armed with a trigger that fires only on a change in who owns what. Each of those choices answers a question the OpenAI proposal leaves open.

A town that owns shares in its only factory reads the pollution report differently.

## FINDING FOUR: AN OIL FIELD CANNOT LEAVE.

The Alaska analogy has a flaw at its centre. Oil is extracted from a fixed place, depletes on a known curve, and cannot move out of the state that taxes it. An AI company's rent depends on continuous reinvestment, can shrink quickly when a competitor catches up, and can relocate.

The larger problem is what the stake does to the state. A government that owns 5 percent of a company has a fiscal interest in that company's value, and the company's value depends in part on how that government regulates it. The Intel terms make this concrete: a passive holder that has agreed to vote with the board is, on shareholder matters, on management's side by contract.

An oil fund never had to decide whether to fine the oil.

## FINDING FIVE: OWNERSHIP IS NOW A CONDITION OF SUPPLY.

The same summer showed equity working in the other direction, as a reason to cut someone off. In August OpenAI announced it would end the coding company Cursor's access to its models after Cursor was acquired by SpaceX. The stated reason was ownership, not conduct: "We are making this choice because we cannot be confident that SpaceX will use our technology within our terms of service, based on our experience with Elon Musk's companies violating contracts."

Cursor's chief executive answered that "OpenAI models serve about 5% of Cursor user traffic," and that Cursor had "trusted their platform to be neutral infrastructure for our business."

That is investment screening, run by a supplier instead of a government. Access to a model now depends on who owns the customer. And the Cursor number shows the limit of the weapon. Because only 5 percent of traffic was at stake, the cutoff cost Cursor little, and every other application company learned to keep its exposure to any one supplier just as small.

## FINDING SIX: EQUITY IS BECOMING THE PRICE OF ACCESS.

Rights holders reached the same instrument from the other side. In December 2025 Disney agreed to "invest $1 billion" in OpenAI alongside a licensing deal letting Sora users create clips with Marvel, Pixar and Star Wars characters. The Hollywood Reporter read it as a sign that studios "may look to embrace" AI companies that already let users infringe their property, and monetise it through licensing.

The logic is the same as the government's. When a right cannot be enforced one generation at a time, buy a seat inside the counterparty's decisions instead. Influence over the generator is worth more than a damages award, and it compounds.

It also lasts only as long as the product it was bought to influence. Three months later OpenAI announced it was shutting Sora down, and Disney "ended its partnership with OpenAI, which included plans for the media conglomerate to take a $1 billion stake". Equity bought for access to one product is a bet on that product surviving.

There is a second risk. An owner's incentive to police the company weakens as its stake grows, and a safeguards agreement without an audit right or a termination right is a press release with a signature.

## FINDING SEVEN: THE PUBLIC WANTS A CLAIM, NOT NECESSARILY A STAKE.

A stake in five companies is one way to give the public a share of AI's gains. It is not the only one. CSIS has argued for an "AI Homestead" policy, a reworking of the nineteenth-century Homestead Act, which transferred 270 million acres of public land to Americans. Its version "should represent a broad policy and private sector commitment to give Americans a fighting chance to own the AI economy's productive frontier before those gains are locked in."

The two designs differ in who holds the claim. A sovereign stake is held by the state on the public's behalf, inside the regulator's own government. A homestead puts the claim in citizens' hands directly. The second is harder to build and has no conflict of interest to manage.

## THE EXPLANATIONS AFTERWARD

Each finding has a plainer reading. Each gets a hearing.

The first explanation was that the offer is public relations, a way to buy political cover.

CNBC's own framing says as much: the proposal is meant to "defuse mounting political pressure." That does not make it trivial. Political cover bought with equity is still equity, and the price the company chose to pay tells you what the cover is worth to it.

The second explanation was that the state can simply tax the rent, and equity adds nothing.

It adds one thing Finding Four argued against. A tax reaches profits earned where the tax applies. A share follows the company wherever it goes. Of all the ways to claim a mobile rent, equity is the one that moves with it.

The third explanation was that none of this will survive Congress.

It may not. But the Intel stake was funded by converting grants already awarded under an existing law. A future stake in an AI company could come the same way, through a subsidy, a contract or a loan converted into shares.

**Objection:** Then the state is just another investor.

**Reply:** And a regulator. Say which one it answers to.

The fourth explanation was the inversion, and a pension-fund trustee would make it. A public stake is the best available cure for regulatory short-termism, not a conflict. A regulator whose public holds shares bears the cost of regulating too hard and the cost of regulating too little, and so has a reason to get it right. Long-horizon public owners, from pension funds to sovereign funds, often behave better as shareholders than the market does.

The case is serious, and the Alaska fund supports part of it: its manager was set up in 1980 "as a quasi-independent state agency," at arm's length from the government that owns it.

**Objection:** Then build the arm's length first.

**Reply:** Before the first share.

The evidence that settles it is structural and can be read before any deal closes. Does the holding vehicle sit outside the agencies that regulate the company? Is its voting passive, or directed, and by whom? Are its holdings disclosed in every rulemaking that touches the company? If the answers are separate, passive and disclosed, the inversion wins. If the stake is held by the same government that writes the rules, with no wall between them, the empty field on the term sheet stays empty.

## THE OTHER KIND OF SOVEREIGN AI

[sovereign-compute](https://github.com/AthenaTheOwl/sovereign-compute) looks at the other way a state can own AI: capacity on its own soil, not shares in someone else's company. It scores national compute programmes against silicon, power, water and talent. Across five programmes announcing 15.3 gigawatts, it finds 9.4 gigawatts that the inputs cannot support, 61 percent of the headline.

The comparison is the point. A stake gives a state a claim on a company's profits and a conflict in its rulebook. Capacity gives it something physical that cannot relocate, and its failure mode is that the announced capacity was never buildable. Both are ways of owning part of AI. Only one of them has to be regulated by its owner.

Claim the rent.

Build the wall before the claim.

TERM SHEET — REMAINING FIELDS

Holding vehicle: outside the agencies that regulate the company.
Voting: passive, by rule, and published.
Disclosure: the stake named in every rulemaking that touches the company.
Exit: a stated rule for selling when the conflict cannot be managed.

The stake is offered. The wall is the part nobody has drafted.
