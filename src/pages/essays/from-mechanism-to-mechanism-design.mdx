A single Vickrey-Clarke-Groves mechanism is a tool. Mechanism design is the discipline of choosing which tools to combine, and recognizing when each one breaks in operation.

[The coordination gap](/essays/the-coordination-gap/) argued that mechanism design recovers joint value without forcing either side to disclose its costs. True in theory. The textbook version of VCG still fails before shipment: full transfers can blow your budget; losing suppliers can collude, and one supplier can bid under several identities, which truthful-reporting incentives do nothing to stop; running the full counterfactual per supplier per round costs real compute; and a planner who can overrule the computed plan voids the dominant-strategy guarantee, which holds only for the allocation rule the transfers are priced against. The math is right; the operating loop needs work.

The operational refinements are themselves design choices. Four worth naming:

**α clipping.** Instead of paying the full VCG transfer `T_i = max(E_i, 0)`, pay `α · T_i` with α between 0 and 1. The math weakens — dominant-strategy incentive compatibility becomes approximate — but the practical anti-gaming pressure survives, and the spend stays bounded. α encodes a posture: how much you trust the vendor base, how much budget you're willing to spend enforcing honesty, and how much approximate-but-cheap you can stomach vs exact-but-expensive.

**Reliability multipliers.** Instead of accepting vendor-stated capacity at face value, the planner system applies a historical-adherence weight. The vendor doesn't have to disclose its cost structure; the planner uses the vendor's past behavior as a prior on stated capacity. This sidesteps the asymmetric-information problem from a different angle — not "make truth-telling worth it" but "the system already knows enough of the truth to discount obvious overpromising." The choice of how much weight history gets is, again, a design decision.

**ε-frontier.** Instead of returning one optimal plan, return the top-K within ε of optimal. The planner picks between optimal-but-thin and slightly-suboptimal-but-robust, and transfers are recomputed consistently for whichever pick lands. ε is the place in the system where human judgment overrides pure optimization. Some categories want ε = 0 (trust the math). Some want ε large (preserve options). The right setting changes as the system matures.

**Decoy demand.** During pilots, inject scenarios with known answers — known-cheap routing, known-fragile suppliers, known-collusion-prone categories — and watch whether the vendor responses match the expected pattern. Catches systematic misreporting that the equilibrium math assumes away. The decoys aren't a feature; they're an audit trail.

The math underneath is the same VCG framework. The operationalization is plural.

Plural is the key word. Procurement gets a *family* of mechanisms parameterized by the four levers above, and the right family member for *which category* and *which season* changes as the system runs. Holiday seasonal volatility wants different α than steady-state purchasing. New-vendor onboarding wants more decoys; mature trusted vendors need fewer. Single-source categories get reliability priors that anchor heavily on incumbent history; categories with three credible alternates can afford more aggressive ε.

You don't pick a mechanism. You pick a family with parameters that adapt.

And then you watch the system run. After a quarter, the data tells you which α was right (did transfers stay in budget? did any group of suppliers move their bids together?), which reliability priors were too generous (which vendors missed commitments at higher rates than the prior expected?), which decoys caught real collusion vs noise (were the misreports concentrated in specific categories?). Each answer feeds back into next quarter's parameter set. The mechanism *is* the practice of running it.

The textbook stops at "this mechanism has dominant-strategy incentive compatibility." The job stops at "this parameter choice fits this category this quarter, and here is the number that will tell us to change it."

The [procurement-negotiation-lab](https://procurement-negotiation-lab.vercel.app/) already carries all four levers as controls: α on the transfer, a reliability weight per counterparty, an ε setting that returns the near-optimal plans with their transfers recomputed, and an Audit Mode that runs the decoys. Setting them is the cheap half. What's the cadence for retuning α? Who owns the reliability-prior update? When does a decoy-detected misreport escalate to contract renegotiation versus algorithmic correction?

Those are product and operating questions. They sit downstream of the mechanism choice and upstream of the actual cost savings. A PMT shipping procurement systems lives in that downstream-of-math, upstream-of-savings layer. The math gets you a seat at the discussion; the discipline of operating the mechanism is most of the actual work.

The four parameters have to be owned by someone with a calendar. Name the owner and the retuning date for each one, or they keep whatever value the pilot happened to leave them at.
