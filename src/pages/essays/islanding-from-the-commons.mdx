INTERCONNECTION QUEUE — ERCOT, AUGUST 2026

Requests in the queue: about 474 GW.
Texas record peak demand: less than a fifth of that. The governor's letter put it as "more than five times."
Share of new requests that are data centres: "Approximately 90 percent."
Response: the governor calls for an audit of every data centre in the queue. The grid operator delays its first study of the new large-load process.
Date by which a new large load can expect power: no field.

The missing field is the reason the largest users of the grid have started leaving it. Interconnection queues are measured in years. Build times for on-site generation are measured in months.

The arbitrage is temporal, not technical.

## FINDING ONE: A WORSE PLANT NOW BEATS A BETTER GRID LATER.

When a shared resource has a multi-year queue, privately provisioning a worse version of it is rational if time-to-revenue dominates efficiency. That is the whole mechanism, and it does not care about the resource.

The deals came in every fuel. Generation built at the data-centre fence line, with the grid as backup. A nuclear startup and a chipmaker announcing reactor-powered compute. A hyperscaler contracting geothermal to skip the line. Co-locate generation with load and a multi-year queue becomes a controllable siting problem.

The name for it is islanding. When a shared utility congests, the largest users defect to dedicated supply and internalise the cost, and the pattern has cousins everywhere the commons is slow: private 5G against carrier spectrum, on-premises inference against congested API tiers, captive water and cooling.

A gas turbine at the fence line is a worse power plant than the grid.

It is a better calendar.

·   ·

Every hospital already has an island.

A diesel set in the basement, tested under load on a schedule, fuel rotated before it goes stale, a transfer switch that has to work the one time in five years it is needed.

Nobody builds it to save money. It exists because the grid's calendar is not the hospital's.

## FINDING TWO: THE COMMONS STARTED ASKING WHO BRINGS THEIR OWN.

Regulators noticed the defection and began rewriting the terms of the commons instead of rationing it by queue.

In December 2025, FERC ordered PJM to revise its tariff for co-located load and behind-the-meter generation, to define the steps a customer must follow to co-locate, and to create new transmission services, signalling that it "expects tailored service options for loads willing and able to curtail use of the transmission system."

In October 2025 the Energy Secretary had directed FERC to consider national rules for connecting large loads, generally anything above 20 megawatts. Among the questions on the table: whether loads that "agree to be flexible and curtail usage should be able to move through interconnection studies faster," and whether large loads and co-located facilities "should pay the full cost" of what they require.

Read together, the direction is plain. The duty to provision shifts onto the marginal entrant. Bring your own supply, bring your own flexibility, or pay the full cost of the upgrades you cause.

Congestion pricing for a commons, paid in generators.

## FINDING THREE: YOU INHERIT THE UTILITY'S RISKS WITHOUT ITS RATE BASE.

Vertically integrate past the permissioned intermediary and you also inherit the utility's risks, outages, maintenance, stranded assets, without its rate base or regulatory cost recovery.

The nuclear version has a date attached. In September 2024, Oracle's chairman told investors the company was designing a gigawatt-scale data centre powered by three small modular reactors, and that "building permits for the three SMRs have already been secured." The report added that he "did not elaborate on the location for the data center or any construction" timeline.

The permits were real. The calendar was the thing being bought, and a first-of-a-kind reactor is the least predictable calendar in energy. If it takes as long to build as the grid connection would have taken to arrive, the skip-the-queue advantage evaporates and the island has paid a premium for the same wait.

An island has to run its own lighthouse.

## FINDING FOUR: THE BILL FINDS YOU THROUGH THE GAS MARKET.

The standard objection to islanding is that it shifts the grid's fixed costs onto the ratepayers left behind. A June analysis from Energy Innovation made a sharper point: the island can raise everyone's bill even if it never touches the grid.

"Counterintuitively, it is data centers' independence from the grid — coupled with their use of natural gas — that will hike energy costs for American homes and small businesses." On-site gas plants add gas demand. Gas supplies 43 percent of U.S. electricity and "gas-fired generators set the price of electricity in most hours," so higher gas prices raise electricity prices for everyone. And the authors noted that regulators "can require utilities to charge grid-connected data centers sufficiently high rates" in a way they cannot for a plant behind the meter.

The island left the grid.

It did not leave the gas market.

## FINDING FIVE: THE ARBITRAGE HAS POLITICAL DURATION, NOT LEGAL DURATION.

On Wednesday 16 September, the House passed a bill directing state utility regulators to consider rules requiring data centres to cover the higher costs of their large-scale power use. The vote was 417 to 3. It still needs the Senate.

A near-unanimous vote on a bill that only asks states to consider something is a signal, and the signal is aimed at the thirty-plus state legislatures already moving data-centre bills, some of them floating moratoria and several revisiting tax credits.

The arbitrage exists because the queue is mispriced, and queues get repriced politically. Every bypass is a bet that the repricing arrives after the plant is paid off. The asset has political duration, not legal duration: a rule change can void it, and rule changes arrive once the arbitrage is visible enough to embarrass someone.

## FINDING SIX: ISLANDING ENTRENCHES WHOEVER CAN AFFORD AN ISLAND.

Bring-your-own-supply is sold as fairness: the entrant who causes the congestion pays to relieve it. Only the well-capitalised can bring their own supply, so a policy framed as fairness becomes a moat, and regulators eventually notice and reverse it.

The compute version has the same shape. Epoch estimated demand for compute growing about ten times a year against three to four times for supply, and bring-your-own-key had already appeared as the private answer to public rate limits.

The list of islanders is short, and it is the same list in every market:

The hyperscaler that can sign a twenty-year reactor offtake.

The fab that can drill its own water.

The lab that can buy its own cluster.

The hospital, with its diesel set, which nobody minds.

·   ·

Leaving the queue is never a private act.

The queue gets shorter for everyone behind you, and the fixed cost of the grid is divided among fewer people. The island is private. Its shoreline is not.

## THE EXPLANATIONS AFTERWARD

The findings have their exits. Each gets its due.

The first explanation was that this is cost-shifting dressed as infrastructure.

Yes. The defector's economics work partly because someone else's rate base absorbs the fixed cost of the grid it left, and Finding Four says it can push costs onto others even from outside the grid. The honest version of the thesis was never that islanding is efficient, only that it is fast, and that speed is currently worth more than efficiency to the people who can afford it.

The second explanation was that the queue is partly phantom. Most projects in interconnection queues eventually withdraw, so much of the observed scarcity would clear on its own, and the bypass premium may be paying to avoid congestion that was never coming.

Also correct, and unanswerable from inside the queue. A phantom queue and a real one look identical to the applicant in year three. The bypass is insurance against not knowing which one you are in, and insurance is usually overpriced to the people who never claim.

**Objection:** Then islanding is a bet on continued dysfunction.

**Reply:** It is a bet that the queue clears slower than the plant builds, and bets on dysfunction have a poor long-run record and excellent medium-run returns.

**Objection:** Which run are we in.

**Reply:** The medium one, until a regulator decides otherwise, and the tell is embarrassment.

The third explanation was that the mechanism is about electricity and the analogies are decoration.

When a shared regulated resource is rationed by queue position instead of price, the highest-value user pays a large premium to leave the queue, accepting worse unit economics for better schedule certainty. Dedicated clinical trial sites. Private port terminals. Captive fab capacity. Private spectrum. The list is the same buyer with a different queue.

The fourth explanation was that federal permitting policy explicitly encourages on-site generation, so the political risk is lower than this essay implies.

Federal policy encourages it. Ratepayers vote in states, and the House vote says Congress has noticed which electorate matters. The two are not the same, and the one with the utility commission is the one that sets the rate.

The fifth explanation was the inversion, and it is the grid operator's view: islanding is a temporary distortion that reform is about to remove. If FERC's large-load rules give flexible loads interconnection studies in sixty days, the queue stops being the binding constraint for anyone willing to curtail, and the islands built to escape it become stranded, expensive, and fuelled by gas.

That outcome is plausible, and the docket is asking exactly that question.

**Objection:** Then build the island anyway.

**Reply:** Temporarily.

The evidence that settles it is the outcome of the large-load rulemaking and the first compliance filings under it. If flexible loads do move through studies in months, the bypass premium collapses for everyone who can curtail, and islanding survives only for loads that cannot, which is a much smaller market. If the reform stalls or the studies stay slow, the islands win the decade. The failure mode sits in the same sentence: an island financed on the assumption that the queue stays broken is a stranded asset the day the queue is fixed, and shared infrastructure usually wins on cost once the congestion clears.

## THE FORM, THE WIRE, AND THE ISLAND

[grid-silicon](https://github.com/AthenaTheOwl/grid-silicon) scores the gap that makes islanding rational: a data centre announced at 1,200 megawatts with 120 energised and 1,080 living on a form. The form is the queue. The island is what a buyer builds when the form stops being an estimate and becomes a calendar.

[robust-siting-lab](https://github.com/AthenaTheOwl/robust-siting-lab) is the siting problem the queue turns into, once a buyer has decided to carry its own supply: three candidate sites scored on expected cost under uncertainty, because a private plant does not get to be wrong about where it stands.

And [power-ppa-forge](https://github.com/AthenaTheOwl/power-ppa-forge) is what happens when three islanders want the same reactor. Three hyperscalers want power off one 1,000-megawatt plant, and none will tell the others what it needs or what it would pay. Islanding at scale is a mechanism-design problem, because the island is shared too.

Leave the queue when the wait costs more than the plant.

Budget for the year the bill finds you.

INTERCONNECTION QUEUE — REMAINING FIELDS

Queue position: surrendered.
Generation: on site, gas, with a reactor promised for later.
Share of the grid's fixed costs now carried by others: see the House vote.
Price of gas for everyone else: a little higher, by the islander's own demand.

The record ends there. The island, it should be said, is still connected to the grid, as backup.
